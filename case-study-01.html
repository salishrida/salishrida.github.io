Case 01 · 2024 Gen AI QSR operations

Cutting engineering tickets by 22% across a 40-store pilot

OVERVIEW

Using Gen AI to help a quick service restaurant client reduce engineering tickets by 22%, by giving store managers step by step troubleshooting guidance in real time.

ROLE
UX Designer
TEAM
Product Manager, Engineers, UX Designer, Account Team
TIMELINE
3 months
STATUS
Shipped, now scaled to 300 stores
01 — THE MOMENT
What the challenge was

In the restaurant stores, equipment failures weren’t rare exceptions, they were part of daily operations. Fryers overheated, shake machines malfunctioned, grills stopped mid-service. Every time this happened, the response was almost automatic: call an engineer.

Across the UK, this translated into thousands of reactive maintenance incidents each year and millions spent on fixing equipment. 

Reactive maintenance was costing the client £8.2M a year, plus 5,500 hours of store closures and £700k in lost sales from equipment breakdowns. And the figure was climbing 10% year on year.

02 — WHAT I FOUND
The mental models we found

Together with the product team, I ran user research with store managers to understand why they were raising engineering tickets for fixes they could often handle themselves.

When equipment fails, there's no playbook.

Frontline restaurant managers had one option when equipment broke: call an engineer. The troubleshooting knowledge existed, buried in hundreds of pages of technical manuals no one had time to search through during a live incident.

"In 10 - 60% of cases, the issues could actually be fixed by the store manager if they had access to the right information at the right time."

That's what the store managers told us.

For the store manager, it wasn't about the costs incurred, it was about the pressure they felt

A stressed manager, standing at a broken machine, one hand occupied, customers waiting. The frustrated manager did not have the time or the patience to go through the manuals and attempt to fix the machinery. 

03 — THE INSIGHT

Managers weren't missing the information on how to fix, they were missing the next step

04 — PROCESS
From chatbot to augmented AI
Iteration 1
The first iteration: a chatbot

The first iteration was the most obvious solution: a chatbot.

Managers ask a question, and the bot responds, with sources and follow-ups.

When tested with the store managers, two gaps emerged. Managers still had to know what to ask, and a paragraph of text is hard to act on mid-shift.

Iteration 2
From chatbot to augmented AI

User feedback moved us away from a chatbot and towards a troubleshooting tool that went beyond chat.

Managers can send text and voice queries, images and videos, and it responds with a step by step fix, shown with images and videos.

01 Works quietly in the background.

Picks up context from what's happening on and off the platform.

02 A guided flow for every question.

Each question gets its own guided flow instead of a long chat thread.

03 Built into tools they already use.

It sits inside BK Hub and connects to Access, the ticketing system stores already use.

05 — WHAT I DESIGNED
Key decisions I made
DECISION 01
Customised to your store

The home screen is set by store location, and shows only appliances present in that store. Managers can select or change their store at any time.

DECISION 02
Diagnose in conversation

The assistant asks targeted questions to confirm the symptoms, then stops talking and returns a step by step flow.

DECISION 03
A guided flow for every question

One step, one screen! Each step gets its own screen, assisted by images and videos of how to perform the step.

DECISION 04
Escalation as part of the journey

When the fix is beyond the manager, managers can escalate to an engineer from inside the app. The call-out ticket is drafted for them, with the appliance, the issue and the steps already attempted. The manager can review it before it's sent.

06 — REFLECTIONS
What I'd do differently.
01
I'd test the solution sooner.

We spent the early weeks proving the working on a chatbot solution that was not the best solution. It was the right first step, but I'd have put a rough iteration in front of the users sooner. User feedback changed the solution direction completely. 

02
Even a single wrong step isn't a small mistake.

A wrong response not only wastes time, but risks hurting the manager or the machine. Next time I'd give importance to the validation process behind each step. 

03
I'd flag the risks associated with the steps up front.

Some steps mean touching hot oil or live wiring, and in the next iteration I'd go through every step for safety, mark the risky ones clearly and tell managers what to watch out for before they start, not after.
